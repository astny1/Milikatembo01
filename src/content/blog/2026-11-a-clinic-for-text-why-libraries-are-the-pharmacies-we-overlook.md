Imagine a clinic without white walls or waiting rooms, a place where people can find words that comfort, challenge, or help them understand what they are going through. A library can offer that kind of space. Its shelves hold stories and ideas that may support someone at the right moment.
This is one way to think about bibliotherapy: the intentional use of reading to support reflection and emotional wellbeing. Libraries are well placed to make reading more accessible and to help people discover books that speak to their interests and experiences.
Librarians select, organise, preserve, and share information. They decide what is available, how it is presented, and how people can find it. This work can also support wellbeing. A librarian might guide a grieving student towards a story about loss, create a welcoming corner where someone can read quietly, or prepare a list of books about anxiety, loneliness, or self-confidence. These actions do not make librarians therapists. They do show how thoughtful collections and welcoming spaces can help people connect with reading.
Books can offer comfort, perspective, and room for reflection. Librarians can help make those possibilities easier to find.
**A Space to Pause**
Every library could consider creating a small, welcoming space for wellbeing reading or a bibliotherapy corner. I prefer to call it a Self-help corner. It might bring together books on personal growth, stories about difficult life experiences, poetry, and other reading chosen to encourage reflection. People may use the space for different reasons. Someone might be looking for personal development; someone else may want a moment of calm, or simply a quiet place to read. The space does not need to be elaborate. What matters is that it feels welcoming and that people know it is there. This is not an extravagance. It is one way a library can serve the people who use it, as well as their academic needs.

**“But People Don’t Read”**
In Zambia, people often describe reading culture as poor. That concern deserves attention, but it need not be the end of the conversation. It can be a starting point.
Some people already read and may welcome the chance to discover books that support reflection and wellbeing. Others might read if they were shown that it can be accessible and enjoyable, that they do not need a degree or a shelf of classics to begin. One story, poem, or helpful book can be enough to spark interest. There are also young, new, and reluctant readers who may find that a book is more than homework. It can be a source of curiosity, recognition, or companionship.

>
> *So the question is not only whether people read. It is also what libraries can do to make reading inviting: one recommendation, one welcoming space, and one library visit at a time.*


**Sometimes, a Book Opens a Door**
Librarians can contribute to emotional wellbeing without taking on the role of counsellors. They can create welcoming reading spaces, curate thoughtful collections, and help readers find material that interests them. Where someone needs professional support, libraries can also help connect them with appropriate services.
A library does not need to reinvent itself. It can build on what it already does: helping people find information, ideas, and stories.

>
> *Sometimes, what someone finds is a book. And sometimes, that book helps them see their experience in a new way.*

**Read. Reflect. Reimagine.**
